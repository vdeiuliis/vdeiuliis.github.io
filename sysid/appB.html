Appendix BPolynomial input–output models

The best-known input–output representation of a discrete-time LTI system is the transfer function in the domain of the \(\Z\)-transform, \(Y(z)=W(z)U(z)\) (Section A.2), familiar from basic control courses together with its continuous-time counterpart in the Laplace domain, \(Y(s)=W(s)U(s)\). Closely related to it is another representation, formulated directly in the time domain: a linear difference equation relating the output samples to the input samples, written compactly by means of the shift operator. This representation has become the standard one in system identification, and also in time-series analysis and forecasting, econometrics and signal processing (see the introduction of Chapter 6). This appendix shows how it is obtained from the transfer function (Sections B.1 and B.2), and then presents the model structures FIR, ARX, ARMAX, OE and Box–Jenkins used in Chapter 6, written in the time domain and in the operator and \(\Z\) domains, together with their relation with state-space models.

B.1 From the transfer function to the difference equation

Positive powers of \(z\). Consider a SISO system with transfer function (Section A.2, (A.6))

\[ W(z)=\frac{N(z)}{D(z)}=\frac{\beta_mz^m+\cdots+\beta_1z+\beta_0}{z^n+\alpha_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+\alpha_0}, \qquad m\le n . \]

The relation \(Y(z)=W(z)U(z)\) can be rewritten as \(D(z)Y(z)=N(z)U(z)\), i.e.

\[ \bigl(z^n+\alpha_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+\alpha_0\bigr)Y(z) =\bigl(\beta_mz^m+\cdots+\beta_1z+\beta_0\bigr)U(z). \]

By the advance property \(\Z\{f(k+1)\}=zF(z)-zf(0)\), multiplying a transform by \(z\) corresponds, up to terms due to the initial conditions, to advancing the signal by one step, and multiplying by \(z^i\) to advancing it by \(i\) steps. Neglecting the initial conditions, the inverse transform gives the difference equation in forward form

\[ y(k+n)+\alpha_{n-1}y(k+n-1)+\cdots+\alpha_0y(k)=\beta_mu(k+m)+\cdots+\beta_1u(k+1)+\beta_0u(k). \]

This is the exact analogue of the continuous-time case, in which multiplying by \(s\) corresponds (with zero initial conditions) to differentiating, and \(Y(s)=W(s)U(s)\) corresponds to a linear differential equation. The equation computes the “future” output \(y(k+n)\) from the \(n\) previous outputs and from the inputs up to time \(k+m\); since \(m\le n\) (properness), no input sample later than \(k+n\) is needed (causality).

Negative powers of \(z\). Dividing numerator and denominator of \(W(z)\) by \(z^n\) (Section A.2),

\[ W(z)=\frac{\beta_mz^{-(n-m)}+\cdots+\beta_0z^{-n}}{1+\alpha_{n-1}z^{-1}+\cdots+\alpha_0z^{-n}} =\frac{B(z)}{A(z)}, \]

where

\begin{align*} A(z)&=1+\alpha_{n-1}z^{-1}+\cdots+\alpha_0z^{-n}=1-a_1z^{-1}-\cdots-a_nz^{-n},\\ B(z)&=\beta_mz^{-(n-m)}+\cdots+\beta_0z^{-n}=b_0+b_1z^{-1}+\cdots+b_nz^{-n}, \end{align*}

that is,

\[a_i=-\alpha_{n-i}\quad(i=1,\dots,n),\qquad b_j=\beta_{n-j}\quad(j=0,\dots,n), \qquad\text{with }\beta_j=0\ \text{for } j>m, \tag{B.1}\]

so that \(b_0=\cdots=b_{n-m-1}=0\) when \(m<n\). The coefficients are renamed because their index changes meaning: \(\alpha_i\), \(\beta_j\) are indexed by the power of \(z\) (in positive powers), whereas \(a_i\), \(b_j\) are indexed by the delay, i.e. by the power of \(z^{-1}\) they multiply; the minus sign in \(a_i\) is the convention of these notes, chosen so that all terms appear with a plus sign on the right-hand side of the difference equation below. In terms of the orders \(n_a,n_b,n_k\) of Chapter 6 (Eq. (6.2)), the input delay is \(n_k=n-m\), while the numbers of coefficients actually present are \(n_a=n\) and \(n_b=m+1\), unless \(W\) has poles or zeros in \(z=0\) (\(\alpha_0=0\) or \(\beta_0=0\)): then the trailing coefficients \(a_n\) or \(b_n\) vanish and \(n_a\) or \(n_b\) are smaller (Remark 6.1). Now \(A(z)Y(z)=B(z)U(z)\) contains only delays, and the delay property \(\Z\{f(k-\tau)\}=z^{-\tau}F(z)\) holds exactly for signals that vanish for \(k<0\) (zero initial conditions). The inverse transform gives the difference equation in backward form

\[ y(k)=a_1y(k-1)+\cdots+a_ny(k-n)+b_{n-m}u\bigl(k-(n-m)\bigr)+\cdots+b_nu(k-n), \]

which is the forward form shifted back by \(n\) steps (\(k+n\to k\)). It expresses the current output as a linear combination of past outputs and of current and past inputs; the smallest input delay that appears, \(n-m\), is the relative degree of \(W(z)\) (the input–output delay). This is the form used in identification and prediction: given the data up to time \(k-1\), it tells how to compute (or predict) \(y(k)\), and it is linear in the unknown coefficients \(a_i\), \(b_j\) (Chapter 6), each of which carries the delay of the sample it multiplies: \(a_i\) multiplies \(y(k-i)\) and \(b_j\) multiplies \(u(k-j)\).

Example B.1: from \(W(z)\) to the difference equation

For the transfer function of Example A.1, \(W(z)=\dfrac1{z-0.5}\):

  • positive powers: \((z-0.5)Y(z)=U(z)\), i.e.

    \[ y(k+1)-0.5\,y(k)=u(k); \]

  • negative powers: \(W(z)=\dfrac{z^{-1}}{1-0.5z^{-1}}\), \((1-0.5z^{-1})Y(z)=z^{-1}U(z)\), i.e.

    \[ y(k)=0.5\,y(k-1)+u(k-1), \]

the same equation shifted by one step; the relative degree \(n-m=1\) appears as the one-step input delay.

Consequences. Every proper rational transfer function is therefore the \(W\) of a polynomial model (ARX, ARMAX, OE, Section B.3) with \(A(z)\), \(B(z)\) as above and, conversely, \(A(q)y(k)=B(q)u(k)\) describes the forced response of every system with transfer function \(B(z)/A(z)\) (Section B.2). Moreover:

  • \(W\) is strictly proper (\(D=0\)) iff \(n_k\ge1\), i.e. there is no term \(b_0u(k)\);

  • common roots of \(A(z)\) and \(B(z)\) cancel: a polynomial model with non-coprime \(A,B\) has a lower-order transfer function, and its parameters are not identifiable (a whole family of \((A,B)\) gives the same \(W\)): model orders should not be over-estimated;

  • for MIMO systems the same construction works with matrix polynomials, e.g. \(A(q)=I-A_1q^{-1}-\cdots-A_{n_a}q^{-n_a}\) with \(A_i\in\R^{q\times q}\) and \(B(q)=B_0+B_1q^{-1}+\cdots\) with \(B_j\in\R^{q\times p}\) (MIMO ARX models, still estimated by least squares), but their parametrization is less straightforward than that of state-space models.

B.2 The shift operator

The difference equations above can be written compactly, directly in the time domain, by means of the forward shift operator \(q\) and of the backward shift (delay) operator \(q^{-1}\), which act on a signal \(f(k)\) as

\[ q\,f(k)=f(k+1),\qquad q^{-1}f(k)=f(k-1),\qquad q^{\tau}f(k)=f(k+\tau),\quad q^{-\tau}f(k)=f(k-\tau). \]

They are linear and time invariant, and compositions of shifts behave like powers: \(q^{-i}q^{-j}=q^{-(i+j)}\). A polynomial operator such as \(A(q)=1-a_1q^{-1}-\cdots-a_nq^{-n}\) acts as \(A(q)y(k)=y(k)-a_1y(k-1)-\cdots-a_ny(k-n)\); products of polynomial operators commute and follow the rules of polynomial algebra. With this notation the two difference equations of Section B.1 read

\[ D(q)\,y(k)=N(q)\,u(k)\quad\text{(forward form)},\qquad A(q)\,y(k)=B(q)\,u(k)\quad\text{(backward form)}, \]

i.e. they are obtained from \(D(z)Y(z)=N(z)U(z)\) and \(A(z)Y(z)=B(z)U(z)\) by replacing \(z\) with \(q\) (and \(z^{-1}\) with \(q^{-1}\)) and the transforms \(Y(z)\), \(U(z)\) with the signals \(y(k)\), \(u(k)\). A rational operator \(G(q)=B(q)/A(q)\) is defined through the difference equation it represents:

\[ y(k)=\frac{B(q)}{A(q)}u(k)\quad\overset{\text{def}}{\iff}\quad A(q)y(k)=B(q)u(k), \]

with zero initial conditions (or, for asymptotically stable \(A\), with the effect of the initial conditions vanishing), so that \(y(k)=\sum_{j\ge0}g(j)u(k-j)\), where \(G(q)=\sum_jg(j)q^{-j}\) is the expansion of \(B/A\) in powers of \(q^{-1}\) (the impulse response).

\(q\) versus \(z\). \(q^{-1}\) is an operator acting on time sequences; \(z\) is a complex variable. The two representations correspond to each other as follows (zero initial conditions):

\[ \underbrace{Y(z)=W(z)U(z)}_{\text{$\Z$ domain}} \quad\iff\quad \underbrace{y(k)=W(q)\,u(k)}_{\text{time domain}}, \qquad z^{\pm1}\ \longleftrightarrow\ q^{\pm1}, \]

where \(W(q)\) is obtained from \(W(z)\) by replacing \(z^{-1}\) with \(q^{-1}\). Formulas written in \(q\) and in \(z\) are therefore formally identical, but \(y(k)=W(z)u(k)\) mixes the two domains and should be avoided. The time-domain form is preferred in identification because it applies directly to the measured sequences, including random noise sequences such as \(e(k)\) for which the \(\Z\)-transform is not the natural tool, and because it makes the unknown coefficients appear linearly in an equation written for each sample (Chapter 6).

Remark B.1 (Alternative conventions). Some textbooks (especially in the Italian tradition of identification and time-series analysis) use the same letter for the delay, writing \(q\,f(k)=f(k-1)\) (or \(z^{-1}\) as an operator, or \(L\) and \(B\) for the lag/backshift operator). In these notes, as in Ljung [5], Söderström–Stoica [7] and MATLAB, the delay is \(q^{-1}\), so that \(q^{-1}\leftrightarrow z^{-1}\). When reading other sources, always check which convention is adopted: with the alternative one, a polynomial “\(A(q)=1-a_1q\)” is our \(A(q)=1-a_1q^{-1}\).

B.3 The model structures

All the structures are special cases of the Box–Jenkins model

\[y(k)=\frac{B(q)}{A(q)}\,u(k)+\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}\,e(k), \tag{B.2}\]

where \(e(k)\) is white noise with zero mean and variance \(\sigma_e^2\) and (with the sign convention of these notes for \(A\))

\[ \begin{aligned} A(q)&=1-a_1q^{-1}-\cdots-a_{n_a}q^{-n_a}, & B(q)&=b_{n_k}q^{-n_k}+\cdots+b_{n_k+n_b-1}q^{-(n_k+n_b-1)},\\ C(q)&=1+c_1q^{-1}+\cdots+c_{n_c}q^{-n_c}, & D(q)&=1+d_1q^{-1}+\cdots+d_{n_d}q^{-n_d}, \end{aligned} \]

with \(A\), \(B\) as in (6.2): the input delay \(n_k\) is embedded in \(B\), which starts from \(b_{n_k}q^{-n_k}\) (e.g. \(B(q)=b_1q^{-1}+b_2q^{-2}\) when \(n_k=1\), \(n_b=2\)). Below, sums over \(j\) run from \(n_k\) to \(n_k+n_b-1\).1 The block diagrams are in Figure 6.1 and the summary in Table 6.1. For each structure we give the operator form, the difference equation and the \(\Z\)-domain description \(Y(z)=W(z)U(z)+H(z)E(z)\) (zero initial conditions).

FIR (finite impulse response). \(y(k)=B(q)u(k)+e(k)\):

\[ y(k)=b_{n_k}u(k-n_k)+\cdots+b_{n_k+n_b-1}u(k-n_k-n_b+1)+e(k), \]
\[ W(z)=B(z)=\frac{b_{n_k}z^{n_b-1}+\cdots+b_{n_k+n_b-1}}{z^{n_k+n_b-1}},\quad H(z)=1 . \]

All the poles are in \(z=0\); the parameters are the first samples of the impulse response (Section 7.4).

ARX. \(A(q)y(k)=B(q)u(k)+e(k)\):

\[ y(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_a}a_iy(k-i)+\sum_{j=n_k}^{n_k+n_b-1}b_ju(k-j)+e(k),\qquad W(z)=\frac{B(z)}{A(z)},\quad H(z)=\frac1{A(z)} . \]

Plant and noise share the denominator: the noise enters “at the input of the dynamics” (the equation error \(e(k)\) is white).

ARMAX. \(A(q)y(k)=B(q)u(k)+C(q)e(k)\):

\[ y(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_a}a_iy(k-i)+\sum_{j=n_k}^{n_k+n_b-1}b_ju(k-j)+e(k)+\sum_{i=1}^{n_c}c_ie(k-i), \]
\[ W(z)=\frac{B(z)}{A(z)},\quad H(z)=\frac{C(z)}{A(z)} . \]

The equation error is a moving average of the white noise.

Output error (OE). \(y(k)=\frac{B(q)}{A(q)}u(k)+e(k)\) (\(C=D=1\)): introducing the noise-free output \(w(k)=\frac{B(q)}{A(q)}u(k)\),

\[ w(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_a}a_iw(k-i)+\sum_{j=n_k}^{n_k+n_b-1}b_ju(k-j),\qquad y(k)=w(k)+e(k), \]
\[ W(z)=\frac{B(z)}{A(z)},\quad H(z)=1 . \]

The noise is white measurement noise on the output; this is the input–output counterpart of the state-space model (8.1) used by MOESP.

Box–Jenkins (BJ). \(y(k)=\frac{B(q)}{A(q)}u(k)+\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(k)\):

\[ W(z)=\frac{B(z)}{A(z)},\qquad H(z)=\frac{C(z)}{D(z)}, \]

with independently parametrized plant and noise dynamics. With \(D=A\) one recovers ARMAX, with \(C=D=1\) OE, and with \(C=1\), \(D=A\) ARX.

Poles and zeros. To find poles and zeros, multiply numerator and denominator by the appropriate power of \(z\). For instance, for the ARX model of Example 6.1,

\[ W(z)=\frac{z^{-1}+0.5z^{-2}}{1-1.5z^{-1}+0.7z^{-2}}=\frac{z+0.5}{z^2-1.5z+0.7},\qquad H(z)=\frac{z^2}{z^2-1.5z+0.7}: \]

poles \(0.75\pm0.37j\) (shared by \(W\) and \(H\)), a zero of \(W\) in \(-0.5\), and a double zero of \(H\) in \(0\). The relative degree of \(W\) (\(2-1=1\)) equals the input delay \(n_k=1\).

B.4 Relation with state-space models

From ARX to state space. Consider a (SISO) ARX model with orders \(n_a,n_b,n_k\) and let \(n=\max(n_a,\,n_k+n_b-1)\) be the largest lag, i.e. the order of the system. To build a realization of dimension \(n\) it is convenient to write both sums up to \(n\), padding with zero coefficients (\(a_i=0\) for \(i>n_a\), \(b_j=0\) for \(j<n_k\) and for \(j>n_k+n_b-1\), Remark 6.1): \(y(k)=\sum_{i=1}^na_iy(k-i)+\sum_{j=0}^nb_ju(k-j)+e(k)\). Define the state components recursively by

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1(k)&=y(k)-b_0u(k)-e(k),\\ x_i(k+1)&=a_iy(k)+x_{i+1}(k)+b_iu(k),\quad i=1,\dots,n-1,\qquad x_n(k+1)=a_ny(k)+b_nu(k). \end{aligned} \]

Unrolling the recursion reproduces the difference equation. Substituting \(y(k)=x_1(k)+b_0u(k)+e(k)\) one obtains the observer canonical form with noise:

\[\left\{\begin{aligned} x(k+1)&=\underbrace{\bmat{a_1&1&0&\cdots&0\\ a_2&0&1&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&&&\ddots&\vdots\\ a_{n-1}&0&\cdots&0&1\\ a_n&0&\cdots&0&0}}_{A_o}x(k) +\underbrace{\bmat{b_1+a_1b_0\\ b_2+a_2b_0\\ \vdots\\ b_n+a_nb_0}}_{B_o}u(k) +\underbrace{\bmat{a_1\\ a_2\\ \vdots\\ a_n}}_{K}e(k),\\ y(k)&=\underbrace{\bmat{1&0&\cdots&0}}_{C_o}x(k)+b_0u(k)+e(k). \end{aligned}\right. \tag{B.3}\]

Without the noise terms this is a realization of \(W(z)=B(z)/A(z)\) (always observable, \(\det(zI-A_o)=z^n-a_1z^{n-1}-\cdots-a_n\), reachable iff \(A\) and \(B\) are coprime).

The innovation form. Model (B.3) is a special case of the innovation form

\[x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+Ke(k),\qquad y(k)=Cx(k)+Du(k)+e(k), \tag{B.4}\]

the most general stationary LTI model with white innovation \(e\) (\(K\) is the steady-state Kalman gain). Eliminating \(e(k)=y(k)-Cx(k)-Du(k)\) gives the predictor form

\[ x(k+1)=(A-KC)x(k)+(B-KD)u(k)+Ky(k),\qquad \hat y(k|k-1)=Cx(k)+Du(k), \]

and, by the matrix determinant lemma, the SISO noise transfer function is

\[ H(z)=1+C(zI-A)^{-1}K=\frac{\det(zI-A+KC)}{\det(zI-A)} . \]

Therefore, in the SISO case, every innovation model is an ARMAX model with \(A(z)\leftrightarrow\det(zI-A)\) and \(C(z)\leftrightarrow\det(zI-A+KC)\) (both divided by \(z^n\)), and

  • ARX corresponds to \(A-KC\) nilpotent (all eigenvalues in \(0\), i.e. \(C(q)=1\)): in (B.3), \(A_o-KC_o\) is the shift matrix with ones on the superdiagonal. The predictor \(\hat y(k|k-1)=[1-A(q)]y(k)+B(q)u(k)\) is then a finite (deadbeat) filter of the data, which is why it is linear in the parameters;

  • ARMAX corresponds to a general \(K\): in observer form, \(K=(a_1+c_1,\dots,a_n+c_n)\T\);

  • OE corresponds to \(K=0\) in the state equation (the noise only enters the output equation), i.e. the model \(x(k+1)=Ax+Bu\), \(y=Cx+Du+e\) of Chapter 8;

  • BJ requires an augmented state of dimension \(n_a+n_d\) (plant and noise dynamics are distinct), with a block-diagonal \(A\).

From state space to polynomial models. Given \(\{A,B,C,D\}\) (SISO), \(W(z)=C(zI-A)^{-1}B+D=\frac{C\operatorname{adj}(zI-A)B+D\det(zI-A)}{\det(zI-A)}\): the denominator gives \(A(z)\) (after division by \(z^n\) and, if the realization is not minimal, after cancelling common factors, Section A.6), the numerator gives \(B(z)\). In MATLAB: [num,den] = ss2tf(A,B,C,D) and tf2ss for the converse (Control System Toolbox; in the System Identification Toolbox also idpoly/idss conversions).

Example B.2: ARX model in state space

For \(y(k)=1.5y(k-1)-0.7y(k-2)+u(k-1)+0.5u(k-2)+e(k)\) (\(b_0=0\)), (B.3) gives

\[ A_o=\bmat{1.5&1\\-0.7&0},\quad B_o=\bmat{1\\0.5},\quad C_o=\bmat{1&0},\quad D=0,\quad K=\bmat{1.5\\-0.7}, \]

and \(A_o-KC_o=\bmat{0&1\\0&0}\) is nilpotent. One checks that \(C_o(zI-A_o)^{-1}B_o=\frac{z+0.5}{z^2-1.5z+0.7}\) and that the simulation of (B.3) coincides with the ARX difference equation for any sequences \(u\) and \(e\) (verified numerically to \(10^{-15}\)).

B.5 Exercises

Exercise B.1. Write the ARMAX model \(y(k)=0.7y(k-1)+u(k-1)+e(k)+0.4e(k-1)\) in innovation form, and verify that the eigenvalue of \(A-KC\) is the root of \(C(z)\).

Exercise B.2. Show that an OE model \(y=\frac{B}{A}u+e\) can be written as an ARMAX model whose polynomials \(A(q)\) and \(C(q)\) coincide. Why is it nevertheless not convenient to estimate it as an ARMAX model?

Exercise B.3. Using the controllable canonical form, find a state-space realization of the ARX model of Example 6.1 and the change of coordinates relating it to (B.3).

Exercise B.4. A MIMO ARX model with \(q=2\), \(p=1\), \(n_a=1\) has \(A(q)=I_2-A_1q^{-1}\) and \(B(q)=b_1q^{-1}\). Write the least-squares problem for \((A_1,b_1)\) and a state-space realization of order \(2\).
