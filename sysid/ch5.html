Chapter 5Introduction to system identification

System identification is the art and science of building mathematical models of dynamic systems from observed input–output data.

L. Ljung [6]

The previous chapters have provided the tools: linear algebra, the singular value decomposition and least squares. In this chapter we turn to the problem that gives the course its name. Identifying a system means building a mathematical model of its dynamics from measured data, rather than deriving it from first principles (physical laws, balance equations), which may be unknown, too complex to be useful, or depend on parameters that cannot be measured directly; in practice the two approaches are often combined. We first explain how dynamic estimation problems differ from the static ones of Chapter 4 and state the identification problem; we then discuss what can (and cannot) be identified from input–output data, the steps of the identification cycle, how to measure the accuracy of a model, and which inputs are rich enough to make identification possible.

5.1 From static to dynamic estimation problems

Up to now we have learned how to approximately (or even exactly) solve static estimation problems described by

\[ y=Ax,\qquad y\in\R^m,\ A\in\R^{m\times n},\ x\in\R^n \quad\leadsto\quad \hat x=A\pinvsym y,\qquad A\pinvsym=V_r\Sigma_r^{-1}U_r\T\ \ (\rank A=r), \]

where the rows of \(A\) and \(y\) are built from measured data (e.g. \(y_i\) and \(t_i\) in curve fitting). We now shift towards estimating dynamic models, in which the current output depends on the past history of inputs and outputs. The identification problem we address can be stated as follows.

Identification problem. Given a training dataset of \(N\) input–output samples

\[\mathcal D_N=\{u(k),\,y(k)\}_{k=0}^{N-1}, \tag{5.1}\]

find, within the class of linear time-invariant (LTI) discrete-time models,

  • an input–output model, i.e. an estimate \(\hat W(z)\) of the transfer matrix, described by the coefficients of its numerator and denominator polynomials (Chapter 6); or

  • a state-space model \(\{\hat A,\hat B,\hat C,\hat D\}\), together with an estimate \(\hat x(0)\) of the initial state if needed. As shown in Section 5.2, these matrices can only be determined up to a change of coordinates in the state space (Chapters 7 and 8).

Restricting the search to linear and time-invariant models is a choice, not a consequence of the data. In general, the problem of associating a dynamic model with the measurements \(\{u(k),y(k)\}\) may have solutions only outside the class of linear models, if the system that generated \(y(k)\) from \(u(k)\) does not admit a linear description (think of saturations, dead zones, hysteresis, or products of signals); in that case nonlinear model classes are needed, such as block-oriented (Hammerstein, Wiener), nonlinear autoregressive (NARX) or neural-network models, which are beyond the scope of these notes. LTI models are nevertheless the standard starting point: many systems behave approximately linearly around an operating point, LTI models are well suited to analysis and control design, and their identification can be carried out with the tools of linear algebra and least squares developed so far. The choice of the model class is one of the steps of the identification cycle (Section 5.3).

The notions of discrete-time LTI systems needed in the sequel (solution of the state equations, impulse response, \(\Z\)-transform and transfer matrices, stability, reachability, observability, polynomial models) are recalled in Appendices A and B; the reader is encouraged to review them now.

5.2 What can be identified from input–output data

Consider the LTI system

\[\left\{\begin{aligned} x(k+1)&=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+\xi(k),\qquad k\ge0,\quad x(0)=x_0\in\R^n,\\ y(k)&=Cx(k)+Du(k)+v(k), \end{aligned}\right. \tag{5.2}\]

with \(u(k)\in\R^p\), \(y(k)\in\R^q\), \(x(k)\in\R^n\). The two noise terms have different origins:

  • \(\xi(k)\in\R^n\) is the process (or state) noise: it accounts for unmeasured disturbances acting on the dynamics and for modelling errors, and it propagates through the state to all future outputs;

  • \(v(k)\in\R^q\) is the measurement (or output) noise: it accounts for sensor errors and affects only the current output sample.

Both are usually modelled as zero-mean random sequences (e.g. white noise). To understand what can be identified at all, in this section we consider the noise-free case \(\xi(k)\equiv0\), \(v(k)\equiv0\); the effect of noise will be addressed in Chapters 6, 7 and 8. The output is then (Appendix A)

\[y(k)=\underbrace{CA^kx(0)}_{\text{free response}} +\underbrace{\sum_{\tau=0}^{k-1}CA^{k-\tau-1}Bu(\tau)+Du(k)}_{\text{forced response}}, \tag{5.3}\]

and its impulse response matrix and transfer matrix are

\[ w(k)=\begin{cases} D, & k=0,\\ CA^{k-1}B, & k>0,\end{cases} \qquad W(z)=\Z\{w(k)\}=C(zI-A)^{-1}B+D . \]

Invariance under changes of coordinates. If we define a change of coordinates \(\tilde x(k)=T^{-1}x(k)\) with \(T\in\R^{n\times n}\) nonsingular, then

\[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde x(k+1)&=\underbrace{T^{-1}AT}_{\tilde A}\tilde x(k)+\underbrace{T^{-1}B}_{\tilde B}u(k),\\ y(k)&=\underbrace{CT}_{\tilde C}\tilde x(k)+\underbrace{D}_{\tilde D}u(k), \end{aligned}\right. \]

and the new impulse response is

\[ \tilde w(k)=\begin{cases}\tilde C\tilde A^{k-1}\tilde B=CT(T^{-1}AT)^{k-1}T^{-1}B=CA^{k-1}B,&k>0,\\ \tilde D=D,&k=0,\end{cases} \]

so that \(\tilde w(k)=w(k)\) for all \(k\), and \(W(z)\) is not affected by changes of coordinates in the state space. Further details are given in Appendix A, see Section A.1.

Two consequences are fundamental for identification.

  1. The data can at most determine \(\{A,B,C,D\}\) up to a similarity transformation \(T\): every identification method returns one realization in some (arbitrary) basis of the state space. Quantities that are invariant under \(T\) (eigenvalues of \(A\), \(D\), the impulse response, the transfer matrix) can be compared with the true ones; the matrices themselves cannot.

  2. Only the part of the system that is both reachable (from the input) and observable (from the output) influences the input–output behaviour (Appendix A, Kalman decomposition). Therefore we are obtaining the minimal state-space representation: we are capturing the observable and reachable (sub)system. The order \(n\) of the identified model is the order of a minimal realization, i.e. the degree of \(W(z)\) after all zero–pole cancellations.

Model classes. Identification requires the choice of a model class \(\mathcal M\) (a set of candidate models parametrized by a vector \(\theta\) or by matrices):

  • white-box models are derived from physical laws, with all parameters known; grey-box models have a physically motivated structure with some unknown parameters; black-box models (the focus of this course) only assume a generic structure. In this notes, as remarked above, we assume a generic LTI structure;

  • input–output polynomial models (ARX, ARMAX, OE, Box–Jenkins, Chapter 6 and Appendix B) are parametrized by the coefficients of polynomials in the delay operator \(q^{-1}\) and are estimated by prediction-error methods;

  • state-space models (5.2) are estimated by realization and subspace methods (Chapters 7 and 8).

5.3 The system-identification cycle

Identification is an iterative procedure, that can be summarized as follows1 (see Figure 5.1).

  1. Design and conduct experiments. Collect data on the system in the most appropriate conditions, trying to obtain data that are representative of the system behaviour: choice of the sampling time, of the input signal (amplitude, frequency content, persistency of excitation, Section 5.5), of the duration.

  2. Pre-process the data. The dataset may contain trends, offsets, outliers, drifts, clear noise, missing samples, which we may want to remove to obtain clean data to work with (e.g. removal of means and linear trends, filtering, resampling). Steps 1 and 2 must deliver data that are informative and representative of the behaviour of the system in the conditions in which the model will be used: only then can one expect the outcome of the following steps to depend mainly on the modelling choices, rather than on the quality of the data.

  3. Build the datasets. Split the whole dataset into training data (only used to learn/estimate the model) and testing data (only used to validate the model, i.e. to understand how the model is able to generalize to unseen data). A heuristic rule is 70–80% training, 30–20% testing (50/50 is also common when data are abundant). The split does not depend on the model class, so it can be done once and for all before any model is estimated. As a practical rule, the pre-processing operations whose parameters are computed from the data (e.g. the mean or the linear trend to be removed) should be computed on the training data only and then applied unchanged to the testing data: otherwise information on the testing data leaks into the model, and the validation becomes optimistic.

  4. Choose a model class. The user may know something about the system in the black box which leads to the assumption that a given model class will be appropriate. Otherwise one should start with simple models and see how they work.

  5. Proceed with estimation. We have training data and a model class \(\mathcal M\): choose a technique that estimates models in that class (LS, PEM, subspace methods). Structural parameters such as the model order are not estimated in this step: models of different orders are estimated and then compared in the validation step (Section 5.4).

  6. Validate the model. After some tests on the training dataset itself, try to understand if the estimated model works fine with testing (unseen) data, so that, according to some measure of error/performance, the error/performance of the model on the testing dataset is not markedly worse than on the training dataset (a slightly larger testing error is normal, while a marked gap is the symptom of overfitting, i.e. of poor generalization) and is satisfactory for the intended use of the model. The latter is the decisive criterion: a model is good if it is accurate enough on unseen data for the purpose it is built for (simulation, prediction, control design, …).

  7. If the model is not good, change the model class (or the order), or the estimation method, and repeat estimation and validation. If no reasonable model performs satisfactorily, the problem most likely lies in the data: as a last resort, revise the pre-processing or repeat the experiments with a better design (steps 1–2).

5.4 Measures of error and accuracy

The identification cycle requires comparing models. Different model classes, orders and estimation methods lead to different models, and the validation step has to decide whether a model is good enough for its purpose and which of several candidates should be preferred. Since no model reproduces measured data exactly (because of noise, unmodelled dynamics and the finite amount of data), the question is not whether the model output coincides with the data, but how large the discrepancy is: on the training data, to assess how well the model fits, and above all on the testing data, to assess how well it generalizes. This requires quantitative measures of error and accuracy, which we now introduce.

Let \(y_i\) be the observed values in an experiment and \(\hat y_i\) the values that the model would produce (e.g. \(\hat y_i=\hat mt_i+\hat q\) for linear regression, or the simulated or predicted output of a dynamic model), \(i=1,\dots,N\), with \(N\) the cardinality of the dataset. Common measures of error and accuracy are:

\begin{align*} \mathrm{MSE}&=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(y_i-\hat y_i)^2 &&\text{mean squared error},\\ \mathrm{RMSE}&=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}=\Bigl(\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\Bigr)^{1/2} &&\text{root mean squared error},\\ \mathrm{NRMSE}_{\mathrm{avg}}&=\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Ny_i} &&\text{not suited if the signal has (close to) zero mean},\\ \mathrm{NRMSE}_{\mathrm{range}}&=\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{\max(y)-\min(y)},\\ \mathrm{FIT}&=100\Bigl(1-\frac{\norm{y-\hat y}}{\norm{y-\bar y}}\Bigr)\ [\%] &&\text{used by MATLAB's \texttt{compare}}, \end{align*}

and many others are possible. The MSE is measured in the squared units of the output (e.g. \(\mathrm{V}^2\) if \(y\) is a voltage), whereas the RMSE has the same physical units as \(y\) (volts, metres, degrees Celsius, …): it can thus be read directly as the typical size of the error made by the model on the output. For the same reason, however, the RMSE depends on the scale of \(y\) and cannot be used to compare errors on signals of different nature or magnitude; the normalized measures \(\mathrm{NRMSE}\) and \(\mathrm{FIT}\) are dimensionless and serve this purpose. Rule of thumb: if the error on the testing dataset is not markedly larger than on the training dataset, \(\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{test}}\approx\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{train}}\) (a slightly larger testing error is normal), the model generalizes well, i.e. there is no overfitting. This alone does not make the model good: a too simple model has comparable but large errors on both datasets (underfitting). As in step 6 of the identification cycle (Section 5.3), the decisive criterion is that the testing error be small enough for the intended use of the model.

These notions (training and testing errors, underfitting and overfitting, choice of the model order) do not depend on the dynamic nature of the model. The simplest setting in which they can be observed is the static polynomial fitting of Chapter 4, where the role of the model order is played by the degree of the polynomial. The aspects that are specific to dynamic models are discussed afterwards.

Example 5.1: choosing the order of a polynomial (solution of Exercise 4.1)

This example solves part (b) of the homework of Exercise 4.1 (Chapter 4): learning a polynomial from data without fixing its order a priori and without overfitting. Data are generated as \(y_i=0.5t_i^3-t_i^2-t_i+1+\varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,0.6^2)\), with \(30\) training and \(30\) testing points drawn uniformly in \([-2,3]\). Polynomials of degree \(d=0,\dots,12\) are fitted by least squares on the training data. Figure 5.2 shows that the training RMSE always decreases with \(d\) (the model classes are nested), while the testing RMSE drops sharply at \(d=3\) and then stays flat or grows: the degree-12 polynomial chases the noise (overfitting) and oscillates between the samples. The testing RMSE is minimal at \(d=5\) (\(0.521\)) but practically identical at \(d=3\) (\(0.540\)): by the principle of parsimony the simplest model with comparable performance is preferred, i.e. \(d=3\). This is also the order selected by the AIC and MDL criteria, which formalize the trade-off between fit and complexity and will be presented in detail in Section 6.6. The estimated cubic, \(0.507t^3-1.023t^2-0.970t+0.947\), is close to the true one. The RMSE \(\approx0.5\) reached for \(d\ge3\) is of the order of the noise standard deviation: no model can do better on unseen data.

Simulation versus prediction. In a dynamic model the output depends on past values of the output itself, so the model output \(\hat y(k)\) can be computed in two different ways. Consider, for instance, a model in the form of a first-order difference equation (Appendix B)

\[ y(k)=a\,y(k-1)+b\,u(k-1), \]

with estimated parameters \(\hat a\), \(\hat b\).

  • The simulated output uses only the input and the past outputs of the model itself:

    \[ \hat y_{\mathrm{sim}}(k)=\hat a\,\hat y_{\mathrm{sim}}(k-1)+\hat b\,u(k-1), \]

    starting from an initial value \(\hat y_{\mathrm{sim}}(0)\); equivalently, \(\hat y_{\mathrm{sim}}=\hat W(q)u\) with \(\hat W(q)=\hat bq^{-1}/(1-\hat aq^{-1})\).

  • The one-step-ahead predicted output uses the measured past output:

    \[ \hat y(k|k-1)=\hat a\,y(k-1)+\hat b\,u(k-1). \]

In prediction the measured value \(y(k-1)\) “resets” the model at each step, so that the errors do not accumulate; in simulation the error made at one step propagates, through \(\hat y_{\mathrm{sim}}(k-1)\), to all the following ones. Prediction errors are therefore typically much smaller than simulation errors and can hide a poor model: for a slowly varying output, even the trivial predictor \(\hat y(k|k-1)=y(k-1)\) (\(\hat a=1\), \(\hat b=0\)), which knows nothing about the system, gives small prediction errors. Validation in simulation on fresh data is the more demanding test. The one-step-ahead predictor of general polynomial models is derived in Section 6.3, where it is also the basis of the estimation method.

Residual analysis. For a model that also describes the noise, the prediction errors \(\varepsilon(k)=y(k)-\hat y(k|k-1)\) on validation data should be (i) white, i.e. their sample autocorrelation \(\hat R_\varepsilon(\tau)\) should be negligible for \(\tau\neq0\), and (ii) uncorrelated with past inputs, \(\hat R_{\varepsilon u}(\tau)\approx0\). Under the whiteness hypothesis, about \(95\%\) of the normalized sample correlations lie in \(\pm1.96/\sqrt N\) (MATLAB: resid).

5.5 Persistency of excitation

Not every input is suitable for identification: a constant input, for instance, cannot reveal the dynamic behaviour of a system at all frequencies. The property that an input must have is captured by the following notion, which involves a block Hankel matrix of input samples.

Definition 5.1 (Persistency of excitation). A signal \(u(k)\in\R^p\), \(k=0,1,\dots\), is persistently exciting of order \(\ell\) if and only if there exists some \(N\) (\(\ge p\ell\)) such that the block Hankel matrix

\[ U_{0,\ell,N}=\bmat{u(0)&u(1)&\cdots&u(N-1)\\ u(1)&u(2)&\cdots&u(N)\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ u(\ell-1)&u(\ell)&\cdots&u(N+\ell-2)}\in\R^{p\ell\times N} \]

is full (row) rank, that is, \(\rank(U_{0,\ell,N})=p\ell\).

The notation \(U_{i,\ell,N}\) (first index \(i\): time of the top-left sample; \(\ell\): number of block rows; \(N\): number of columns) will be used throughout Chapters 7 and 8; \(Y_{i,\ell,N}\) denotes the analogous matrix built with the outputs. For stationary stochastic signals an equivalent asymptotic definition requires \(\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1NU_{0,\ell,N}U_{0,\ell,N}\T\succ0\) [1].

Example 5.2: persistency of excitation of common signals

  • A step \(u(k)=1\) gives a Hankel matrix with all entries equal to one: rank one, so it is PE of order 1 only.

  • A sinusoid \(u(k)=\sin(\omega k)\), \(0<\omega<\pi\), satisfies \(u(k+2)=2\cos\omega\,u(k+1)-u(k)\): every row of the Hankel matrix beyond the second is a combination of the first two, so it is PE of order 2 (and not 3). A sum of \(s\) sinusoids with distinct frequencies in \((0,\pi)\) is PE of order \(2s\).

  • A realization of white noise (or a pseudo-random binary sequence of suitable period) is PE of any order \(\ell\) for \(N\) large enough: this is why these signals are the standard choice for identification experiments.

Intuitively, a signal PE of order \(\ell\) “contains at least \(\ell\) frequencies”, and can be used to identify models with up to about \(\ell\) parameters in the input–output relation.

Stacking inputs and outputs,

\[ \bmat{U_{0,\ell,N}\\ Y_{0,\ell,N}}= \bmat{u(0)&\cdots&u(N-1)\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ u(\ell-1)&\cdots&u(N+\ell-2)\\ \hline y(0)&\cdots&y(N-1)\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ y(\ell-1)&\cdots&y(N+\ell-2)}, \]

one obtains a matrix whose columns are trajectories of length \(\ell\) of the system. The following result, known as Willems’ fundamental lemma (J. C. Willems; other key names in this line of research are I. Markovsky and B. De Moor), is nowadays very used in the literature on data-driven control (see e.g. De Persis and Tesi, “Formulas for data-driven control: stabilization, optimality and robustness”, IEEE TAC 2020 [15], and [14, 16]).

Theorem 5.2 (Willems’ fundamental lemma, state-space version). Let the system (5.2) (noise free) be reachable, let \(u\) be persistently exciting of order \(\ell+n\), and let \(X_{0,N}=[x(0)\;x(1)\;\cdots\;x(N-1)]\) be the corresponding state sequence. Then

\[ \rank\bmat{U_{0,\ell,N}\\ X_{0,N}}=p\ell+n . \]

If moreover the system is observable and \(\ell\ge n\), also \(\rank\bmat{U_{0,\ell,N}\\ Y_{0,\ell,N}}=p\ell+n\), and every input–output trajectory of length \(\ell\) of the system is a linear combination of the columns of this matrix.

In other words, a single, sufficiently rich experiment contains all the information about the system, in the form of the column space of a data Hankel matrix. The same kind of rank condition is the enabling assumption of the MOESP algorithm (Lemma 8.1).

5.7 Implementation in MATLAB and Python

The validation measures and a numerical check of persistency of excitation take a few lines of core MATLAB:

MATLAB
rmse   = @(y, yh) sqrt(mean((y - yh).^2));
nrmse  = @(y, yh) rmse(y, yh) / (max(y) - min(y));
fitpct = @(y, yh) 100*(1 - norm(y - yh)/norm(y - mean(y)));
% persistency of excitation of order l: rank of the block Hankel matrix
u = sin(0.3*(0:199)');  l = 3;  N = numel(u) - l + 1;
H = hankel(u(1:l), u(l:end));           % l x N Hankel matrix (scalar input)
fprintf('rank = %d (PE of order %d requires %d)\n', rank(H), l, l);
% random training/testing split (70/30) of a static dataset
m = 100; idx = randperm(m); itr = idx(1:70); ite = idx(71:end);

With the System Identification Toolbox (not available in GNU Octave), data are handled through iddata objects and the cycle of Figure 5.1 reads:

MATLAB
data = iddata(y, u, Ts);                 % output, input, sampling time
data = detrend(data);                    % remove means / linear trends
ze = data(1:700);  zv = data(701:end);   % training (estimation) / testing (validation)
m  = arx(ze, [2 2 1]);                   % estimation (see the PEM chapter)
compare(zv, m);                          % simulation fit on validation data
resid(zv, m);                            % whiteness and independence tests

In Python the same metrics are in code/python/sysid.py (rmse, nrmse_range, fit_percent); Figure 5.2 is produced by the function poly_order_selection of experiments.py:

Python
import numpy as np
f = lambda t: 0.5*t**3 - t**2 - t + 1
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
ttr = rng.uniform(-2, 3, 30); ytr = f(ttr) + 0.6*rng.standard_normal(30)
tte = rng.uniform(-2, 3, 30); yte = f(tte) + 0.6*rng.standard_normal(30)
for d in range(13):
    th, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(np.vander(ttr, d + 1), ytr, rcond=None)
    V = np.mean((ytr - np.vander(ttr, d + 1) @ th)**2)
    e_te = np.sqrt(np.mean((yte - np.vander(tte, d + 1) @ th)**2))
    aic, mdl = np.log(V) + 2*(d + 1)/30, np.log(V) + (d + 1)*np.log(30)/30
    print(d, np.sqrt(V), e_te, aic, mdl)
Further Study: cross-validation and informative experiments

\(K\)-fold cross-validation. When data are scarce, a single training/testing split wastes information and gives a noisy estimate of the generalization error. In \(K\)-fold cross-validation the data are divided into \(K\) blocks; each block is used once for testing while the model is trained on the other \(K-1\), and the \(K\) testing errors are averaged [18]. For dynamic systems the blocks must be contiguous in time (and the initial conditions of each block handled properly), otherwise the correlation between neighbouring samples makes the testing data not really “unseen”.

Informative experiments. A dataset is informative with respect to a model class if it allows one to distinguish between any two models of the class that have different input–output behaviour [5]. For open-loop experiments with quasi-stationary signals, informativity is guaranteed by persistency of excitation of sufficient order; in closed loop (the input is generated by a feedback controller) informativity may be lost even with rich reference signals, which is the root of the difficulties of closed-loop identification.

Willems’ lemma in its behavioural form states that, for a controllable system with lag \(\ell_0\) and an input PE of order \(\ell+n\), the image of the data Hankel matrix equals the set of all length-\(\ell\) trajectories. It underlies data-driven predictive control (DeePC) and the direct design of controllers from data [15, 16].
