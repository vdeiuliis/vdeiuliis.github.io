---
title: "Peer review was already broken; LLMs just finished the job"
date: 2026-10-25
---

Peer review has been broken for a long time, even though we have not yet found a better way to evaluate research output. Over the years, *publish or perish* has produced an obvious paradox: most authors (sadly, including the youngest ones) end up writing more papers than they read. Most researchers feed lines of research destined to have little or no global impact, not for lack of talent, but because following that path is the fastest and least risky way to build a career. Trying to open a new path, or to explore a less-travelled one, does not pay off: neither in terms of the number of papers one can produce in a given amount of time, nor in terms of the immediate resonance those papers can have among their (few) readers, since very few people read anything outside their own narrow area of interest and expertise. Let me be clear: I am not moralizing here. I am writing about this because I, too, have felt that this was the sensible thing to do, given how things work. I am certainly not innocent!

One way to be encouraged (I would say: forced) to read outside one's immediate field has always been to accept review requests somewhat distant from one's own interests. A reviewer does not always need to be the world's leading expert on a topic: it is often enough (and, for the authors, quite insidious!) that they are fairly competent in the broader research area, without necessarily being an expert on the specific subject of the paper. This can reveal problems and opportunities that are not always visible to a narrow specialist.

The enormous impact that LLMs are having on researchers' productivity has dealt two further blows to peer review, and to scientific publishing in general. First, the already huge rate of production is growing at an even more unsustainable pace: one can now write *n* papers in the time it used to take to write one. This hyper-productivity is tempting, and no one wants to waste time reading papers anymore, given that hardly anyone is willing to spend time even writing them. The result is that, while the number of papers to be reviewed grows out of all proportion, the number of researchers willing to accept review requests seems to keep shrinking, and finding them has become extremely hard. We thus reach the paradox of authors who submit 5, 10, even 15 co-authored papers to a conference (a true absurdity, which should be subject to serious restrictions) while reviewing zero papers for that same conference. This is an unacceptable lack of respect for research ethics.

Then there is a further point: it is hard to find reviewers who are more competent, and better informed about the existing literature, than the latest frontier models. If you think about it, it could not be otherwise: they have actually read the literature, since they were not too busy producing it over the last few years! Moreover, the growth in the technical and scientific capabilities of LLMs over the last few months is plain for all to see, and the temptation to get their help with a review is entirely understandable. And, all the more so in a research world dominated by haste, reviews produced by LLMs end up being less half-hearted and rushed than human ones, even when the human reviewers are competent and professional. The result, again plain for all to see, is that LLMs are becoming not only the main authors of the scientific literature, but also its main reviewers. They are, quite literally, marking their own homework, and the only real reason an LLM reviewer can find anything to say to an LLM author is that the time it takes for a paper to go through review allows the models to advance: the reviewer is simply a slightly more competent version of the author, that is, of itself. We are facing a future in which the scientific literature is produced and judged by artificial intelligence.

And there is nothing very strange about this, except the anachronism of the ban on (or the low tolerance for) the use of LLMs in peer review. Such a ban does make sense, at least within the current framework, dominated by publishers' even more anachronistic copyright regime, and given the way information is used by the for-profit companies developing frontier models, which also calls for serious regulation. We are facing an absurdity: today authors must declare their use of LLMs, while reviewers are strictly forbidden from using them (a rule evidently circumvented by a huge share of reviewers in the real world, where things move faster than laws and regulations). And yet, what use of an LLM could make more sense than that of an "expert" who reads research outputs and spots their flaws? In an ideal, well-regulated world, publishers themselves should encourage the use of advanced frontier models for reviewing. One solution to the copyright problem could be the use of open-weights models, which publishers could scrutinize and control. Such models would not even need to be frontier-grade if used for a preliminary screening aimed at reducing the workload of human reviewers, who might then accept the task as less of a burden. The likely result would be higher-quality published papers and, without doubt, less hypocrisy.
