---
title: "Studying and teaching in the age of LLMs"
date: 2026-11-01
---

This is the second academic thought I have written on my website, and once again it concerns the impact of LLMs on my work. It is a sign of the times: in the corridors of the university departments I frequent, people talk of little else than this and of its incredible impact on our work.

The point I would like to address today is how to adapt a way of studying and teaching that has developed and consolidated over centuries to such a sudden change. I am not saying that the traditional lecture was necessarily the optimal way to teach: I have highly respected colleagues who already considered it an outdated and stale format, and the whole assessment system based on "giving a grade" to a student after a test has always been viewed with suspicion and repeatedly rethought (I recommend that anyone reading this watch a couple of enlightening and entertaining TED Talks by Ken Robinson: [this one](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wX78iKhInsc), more relevant to the topic of this post, and [this one](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iG9CE55wbtY), which is just as worth watching, and very funny).

The point is that the entire system of producing teaching material (lecture notes, slides, books), complete with examples and, in STEM disciplines, computer implementations, seems totally anachronistic today, if students can question, step by step, a seemingly omniscient oracle that has read and compared far more sources than their teacher knows. And this is true even though the teacher may well have chosen those sources more wisely than the LLM, picking a text they consider more effective for teaching over one that an LLM might draw from its corpus, which could prove too technical or somewhat cryptic as a first source for a student.

How, then, can we produce teaching material that keeps up with the times? Can we still assume that the "average" student is willing to rely on a reference textbook, or perhaps on course notes painstakingly assembled and fine-tuned over the years? Or will the temptation always prevail to take a few distracted notes and, based on them, question the oracle of the moment, abandoning the sources altogether? And moreover: how can teaching material adapt, even just in its form, to new media, and become more interactive and more stimulating for learning?

I have thought about this a lot. Some time ago, after a few failed attempts, I decided to try to digitize and expand, with the help of Claude, the notes I had put together a few years ago for a System Identification course for master's students in L'Aquila. I had been planning this for a long time, but transcribing everything in LaTeX and carrying out a thorough revision seemed beyond my reach at this stage. With the help of a frontier multimodal LLM, this suddenly becomes feasible, with an obvious benefit: any teacher who has collected poorly organized or poorly revised teaching material over the years can, with minimal effort, obtain a version far more ready to be shared and studied by students. A version that has been examined by a careful reviewer, who will have corrected errors and inaccuracies and, under close guidance, proposed additions where needed.

However, I decided to go one step further, on which I am progressing more slowly but in a way I really enjoy: building a web-based, interactive version of the course notes. I no longer teach this course, but I consider it useful material, to be enriched over time, simply for myself or for anyone who might one day want to consult it. Right now, building an interactive version seems to me one of the best things one can do to "refresh" the way we use "static" sources (books, lecture notes, etc.) and to make studying them more stimulating for students. What is taking shape for this particular course can be found [here](/sysid/). It is partial, only partly revised (up to Chapter 5), and certainly open to major improvements and additions, but it seems to me a good product that, as a student, I might have preferred to a simpler static version (which also exists and can be printed). It will certainly not be enough to stem the strong temptation to study in a quicker (though still stimulating) way, by bombarding a chatbot with questions (I hope this is not a false friend of the Italian *bombardando*), but it could be a good alternative.

There remains the crucial question of how assessment (the exam) should change. It seems impossible to give the written part (solving exercises) the same weight it has had over the last few decades: students know that an LLM can nowadays easily solve any exam problem or exercise, and I fear that the temptation to look for a shortcut is almost irresistible for many. An oral exam seems better suited to testing a student's knowledge, but it does not lend itself well to calculations and exercises, and of course it takes a great deal of time with very large classes. Assessment distributed throughout the year, with exercise sessions and checks on the understanding of fundamental concepts at fixed deadlines, seems an interesting alternative, but this is truly an open problem, on which opinions can differ widely, also depending on the subject being taught.

These are incredibly stimulating and fascinating times, in which a student's temptation to think that everything is "useless" is almost understandable. It is up to teachers to find strong arguments and new ways of teaching that answer this doubt convincingly, and renew the most beautiful job in the world: passing on knowledge.
